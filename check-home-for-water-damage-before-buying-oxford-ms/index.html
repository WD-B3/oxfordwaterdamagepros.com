How to Check a House for Water Damage Before You Buy

A good walkthrough finds the clues sellers do not mention. Here is what to look at, what to ask and when to bring in a professional.

By the Oxford Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026-11-10

Why a walkthrough should include water

Water damage is one of the most expensive problems to find after closing. A stain can be painted over in an afternoon, and a soft floor can be hidden by new carpet. A careful buyer looks for patterns rather than single marks, because one old stain may be harmless while three in a row point to a recurring leak.

None of this replaces a licensed home inspector. It helps you ask better questions and decide whether to request a more detailed inspection before you commit.

Start outside the house

Walk the perimeter first. Look at the ground, because water that slopes toward the foundation tends to find its way in. Check that gutters are attached and that downspouts discharge away from the walls. Look for stains, cracks or damp patches at the base of exterior walls, and for vegetation growing against the siding.

Look up at the roof line. Missing shingles, sagging sections and stained soffits are signs of past or current leaks. If the house has a crawl space, look at the vents and the access hatch, and read the crawl space page for what a wet one looks like.

Ceilings and walls

Inside, look at every ceiling with the lights on and then with a flashlight held at a low angle. Shadows reveal bulges and rings that overhead light hides. Brown or yellow rings with darker edges are a classic sign of a repeated leak.

Press on the drywall near corners, windows and under sinks. It should feel firm. Soft, crumbly or cold spots suggest moisture behind the paint. The wall and drywall page explains what those signs mean.

Floors

Walk across every room slowly. Floors that feel spongy, uneven or cold are suspect. On hardwood, look for cupping and dark stains between boards. On carpet, press down near walls and corners, and sniff for musty odor. A fresh coat of paint on only one wall or a single new patch of flooring is worth asking about.

Under sinks and around fixtures

Open every cabinet that holds plumbing. Look for water rings, swollen particleboard, rust on pipes and active drips. Check around toilets for soft flooring and around tubs for loose tile and gaps in caulk. Run the water for a minute and watch the pipes for drips.

Smell, humidity and the HVAC

A house that smells musty even when it is clean may have hidden moisture. Check the air conditioner closet and the drain line for stains and standing water. A leaking condensate line is a common source of damage, and the AC leak page explains how it happens.

Look at the attic and utility areas

Open the attic hatch if there is one and look at the underside of the roof deck for dark stains, rusted nail tips and damp insulation. Check around vents, chimneys and skylights, because these are the places where roofs most often leak. In the utility area, look at the water heater base, the floor drain and the walls near the washer connections for rust, rings and soft spots.

Ask the right questions

Ask the seller whether the home has ever had a leak, a flood or an insurance claim for water damage. Ask what was repaired and who did the work. Ask for receipts and permits for plumbing or roofing repairs. A seller who answers openly is usually a better sign than a house that looks perfect.

When to call a professional

If you find a stain, a soft spot or a smell, a moisture reading can show whether it is active. A reading on the day of the walkthrough is only a snapshot, but it can tell you whether the area is wet now. A professional can also tell you what drying or repair would involve so you can use that when negotiating.

What to do if you find damage

You can ask the seller to repair it before closing, ask for a credit, request a deeper inspection or walk away. The decision depends on how much damage there is, how much you trust the explanation and how the repair affects your insurance.

Buying near campus or in a newer subdivision

Oxford has older homes close to the Square and newer homes spread across Lafayette County, and each has its own water risks. Ask the City of Oxford Building Department at 107 Courthouse Square about permits on any past renovation. If the home is in a mapped flood area, check the FEMA flood map for the address and ask your insurer about flood coverage before you close.

Questions about this topic

Can I tell if a stain is old or new?

Sometimes. A dry, faded stain may be old, and a damp or spreading one is active. A moisture meter gives a more reliable answer than touch.

Should I ask for a water damage history?

Yes. Ask the seller whether there have been leaks, floods or insurance claims, and ask for receipts for repairs.

Is fresh paint a warning sign?

Not by itself, but a single freshly painted patch on a ceiling or wall is worth asking about.

Does a home inspection cover hidden moisture?

An inspector looks for visible signs and may use a moisture meter. Ask what is included before you hire one.

What if I find damage after closing?

Document it with photos, stop the source and call for a moisture inspection. Check your closing paperwork and ask your insurer what is covered.
