Water Damage Categories 1, 2 and 3 Explained

Not all water is equal. The category tells you how risky the water is and what the cleanup has to include.

By the Oxford Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026-11-10

Why categories exist

The restoration industry classifies water by how contaminated it is. The classification comes from the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, which is the industry reference for professional water damage restoration. The categories help crews and homeowners decide what protective gear is needed and which materials can be dried and kept.

The category is not about how the water looks. Clear water can be contaminated, and cloudy water can be harmless. It is about the source and about what the water has touched.

Category 1: clean water

Category 1 water comes from a sanitary source such as a supply line, a faucet, a tub overflow with no contaminants or a broken water heater line. It poses little risk at the start. Most materials can be dried in place.

Time matters. Category 1 water that sits can pick up contaminants from the materials it soaks and become Category 2. That is why speed is part of the classification and not just a rule of thumb. A burst pipe caught quickly is a good example of a Category 1 loss.

Category 2: gray water

Category 2 water has some contamination that could cause illness if consumed or contacted. Typical sources are washing machine and dishwasher discharge, toilet overflow with urine but no feces and water from a sump pump. It is treated with more care.

Porous materials such as carpet pad and drywall that have been wet with gray water are often removed. Hard surfaces can be cleaned and disinfected. The appliance leak page covers the situations that commonly produce it.

Category 3: black water

Category 3 water is grossly contaminated. Sewage, floodwater from rivers and streams, and water from outside that has been standing are Category 3. It can carry bacteria, viruses and other harmful material. People and pets should stay out.

Category 3 loss calls for protective gear, containment and removal of porous materials. The sewage backup page and the flood damage page describe how crews handle it.

Classes of water damage

There is a second scale called class, which describes how much water the materials absorbed. It ranges from class 1, with little absorption, to class 4, which involves dense materials such as hardwood and concrete holding deep moisture. Class decides drying equipment and time. Category decides contamination and what must come out.

How a category can change

A clean loss can move to a worse category if it sits for days or touches contaminated surfaces. A dishwasher overflow that is mopped promptly may stay a lower category. A basement that fills with ground water is treated as Category 3 from the start.

What you can do

Do not assume water is clean because it looks clear. Keep people and pets out of any water that came from a drain, the ground or outside. Do not use household cleaners to treat a Category 3 loss on your own. Photograph the area and note the source for your records.

Examples from real homes

A failed ice maker line that sprays a kitchen floor is usually Category 1 if mopped quickly. A washing machine that overflows with soapy water is Category 2. A toilet that backs up from the main line is Category 3, and so is a basement that takes in water from a creek or a street. Notice that the room is not the deciding factor.

The source and the contact time are.

If you cannot tell, treat the water as the more serious category until a crew has assessed it. Being careful costs very little, and guessing wrong can make people sick.

Why contact time matters

Contact time is how long the water has been in contact with a material. Even Category 1 water becomes more of a concern as contact time grows, because materials hold moisture and organisms grow. A wet carpet pad left for several days is treated with more caution than one removed within hours. For this reason, crews ask when the water started and how long it ran.

Why it matters for your insurer

The category can affect coverage and how the claim is described. A supply line failure may be treated differently from a sewer backup or flood. Ask your insurer which of these your policy covers.

When to bring in a professional

Category 2 and 3 losses are best left to a trained crew. Even a Category 1 loss benefits from professional drying when walls, insulation or flooring are involved, because the moisture is not visible. If you are not sure what type of water you have, call and describe the source.

Floodwater and storms in Oxford

Heavy rain in Lafayette County can turn a roof leak, a street flood or a backed-up drain into a mixed loss, so categories can overlap in a single house. In February 2019, heavy rain eroded roads and cracked water lines in Oxford, and the city issued a precautionary boil notice. After storms, treat water that came from outside as contaminated until a crew has looked at it.

Questions about this topic

What category is a burst supply line?

Usually Category 1 at first. It can degrade if it sits.

Is rainwater Category 1?

Not usually. Rain that enters through a roof and runs through insulation is often treated with more care. A crew can assess it.

Is flood water always Category 3?

Floodwater from rivers, streams and the ground is classified as Category 3.

Can I clean Category 3 water myself?

It is not recommended. Protective gear and disinfection are needed, and porous materials are removed.

Who decides the category?

A trained crew assesses the source and the conditions, often with your help describing what happened.
